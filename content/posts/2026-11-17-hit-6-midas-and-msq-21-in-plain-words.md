A migraine appointment is short. The doctor has a few minutes, and you have years of bad days to describe. So many clinics use a questionnaire. It turns the bad days into a number that a doctor can compare over time.

Three questionnaires come up again and again: HIT-6, MIDAS and MSQ. I know them because [Migraine.Club](https://www.migraine.club) builds its doctor summaries on them. This post explains each one in plain words. I am an engineer, not a doctor, so I will stay with how the tools work.

This is not medical advice. Talk to your doctor.

## HIT-6: how much migraine gets in the way

HIT-6 stands for Headache Impact Test, with six questions. Each question asks how often headache affects something in your life. Examples are pain, daily activities, and feeling tired or irritated. You answer from "never" to "always".

Each answer gives points, and the points add up to a total between 36 and 78. A higher number means a bigger impact. Clinical trial documents, such as this [statistical analysis plan](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/39/NCT05452239/SAP_001.pdf), describe the scoring and the usual four groups. The groups go from "little or no impact" (49 or less) to "severe impact" (60 or more).

HIT-6 looks at a recent period, about the last four weeks. It does not count your attacks. Two people with the same number can live very different lives. That is fine, because the test measures impact, not frequency.

## MIDAS: how many days you lose

MIDAS stands for Migraine Disability Assessment. It has five questions about the last three months. The first questions ask how many days you could not work or study, or how many days you did much less because of headache. Other questions cover housework and free time.

You add up the days. The total puts you in one of four grades, from little or no disability to severe disability. The lowest grade is 0 to 5 days. The highest is 21 days or more.

I like MIDAS because the unit is easy to understand. A day is a day. But look at the long window. Three months is a long time to remember. If you answer from memory, I think the number can easily be too low or too high.

## MSQ: how migraine feels in daily life

MSQ means Migraine-Specific Quality of Life Questionnaire. The version I know has 14 questions, and doctors often call it version 2.1. It looks at the last four weeks.

The questions fall into three groups. One group asks how migraine limits your daily tasks. One group asks how migraine stops you from doing things, for example when you cancel a plan or stay away from people. The last group asks about feelings, such as frustration. A [validation study of the Greek version](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11250746/) describes this structure.

Each group gets a score from 0 to 100. Here the direction flips. A higher MSQ score means a better quality of life. I find this easy to get wrong, so read the arrow on every chart.

## One question each, side by side

The three tests are not rivals. They ask three different questions.

HIT-6 asks "how bad is the impact?" MIDAS asks "how many days did I lose?" MSQ asks "how does migraine change my life?"

A doctor may use one, two or all three. Some clinical trials use them as well, to see if a treatment changes the numbers. I will not say which test is "best". It depends on the question the doctor wants to answer.

## Why memory makes these tests weak

All three tests ask you to remember. That is the weak point, and I think it is the part most people miss.

I think memory favours the worst attack and the most recent week. The mild days fade. A form in a waiting room, filled in within five minutes, may tell you more about that day than about the last three months. This is my opinion, not a result from a study.

A diary fixes this in a simple way. You write each attack down on the day. You note the date, the length, and whether you could work. At the end of the month the facts are already there. The questionnaire becomes a check of the diary, not a guess.

That is the idea behind the summaries in Migraine.Club. The app does the counting, so you can focus on the answers. Be careful with any app that shows a score, including mine. A score is only as good as its inputs, so I label how sure the app is.

## What to bring to the appointment

If you have an appointment soon, keep the list short.

- Bring your diary, or a list of attack dates for the last three months.
- Count the days you lost to migraine, and note the number.
- Write down how many days you took medicine, because the doctor will ask. Do not change any dose on your own.
- Write your two biggest worries.

Do not try to match a "good" score. The numbers are for your doctor, not for a prize. If you want to learn more about migraine in general, the [International Headache Society](https://ihs-headache.org/) is a good place to start.
